**me:**

I need to discuss something with you. I'm working on a game that I'm making similar to *The Long Drive*. It features a procedurally generated desert and a narrow road running 40,000 km through the middle of it. The desert gradually changes color as you drive, the road winds, and hills give way to ravines. You’ll encounter a wide variety of cars and can test their engines and suspension/handling characteristics. But I want to give the player something enjoyable and fun, beyond just the overall aesthetics. For example, you could turn on the radio in the car—maybe it wouldn’t be too hard to make the landscape (perhaps only visible in the distance, not real, just decorative) change in time with the music. Add some simple but enjoyable events or visual effects—something that would make the player want to drive a little further down this endless road, some kind of special feeling. I don’t want survival, thirst, hunger, or losing control due to a flat tire; on the contrary, I want positive emotions. What do you suggest?

**agents:**

It seems to me that there’s a very strong foundation here for a game that isn’t about “getting there,” but about enjoying the journey. 40,000 km of road in and of itself creates an unusual promise: there doesn’t necessarily have to be a reward just over the next hill—but something beautiful, strange, or cozy might happen.

I wouldn’t turn this into a set of random encounters in the conventional sense. It’s better to create several systems that periodically cause the world to react to the player’s presence.

Radio as a way to add a bit of “magic” to the world

The idea with music is excellent. And it’s not necessary to literally animate the actual terrain to the beat of FFT—it can be done much more simply.

For example, the world has a decorative distant layer: mountain silhouettes, mirages, clouds, the sun, haze, stars, and roadside poles. Each track or radio station has several parameters: energy, dreaminess, warmth, and rhythm.

And then the music very slowly influences the world. With a calm track, the mountains in the distance become long and smooth, the horizon widens, and the clouds drift more slowly. With an energetic track, sharp cliffs appear, the roadside posts start to move in time with the rhythm, and the road winds a bit more actively. With strange electronic music, the horizon might split in two, distant geometric structures might appear, or a huge moon might emerge.

It’s especially nice if the effect doesn’t kick in instantly. You drive for a couple of minutes to a song and suddenly notice: “Wait a minute, were the mountains always like that?..”

This is much more magical than an equalizer on the horizon.

Let the road sometimes do something just for you

I’d introduce rare states of the world—so-called “road moods.”

Not “the event has begun!”—no UI at all. Just a seed + distance + time of day sometimes combine to create a special scene.

For example, for about five minutes, the road becomes perfectly straight and leads straight into a huge setting sun. Or a very long, gentle descent begins, where you can turn off the engine and coast for a few minutes while listening to the radio. Or thousands of tiny white flowers appear along the roadside. Or the road suddenly winds through a red desert with pitch-black cliffs.

I also like the idea of the perfect stretch of road. Very rarely does the generator create 10–20 km of exclusively pleasant, smooth turns and elevation changes. No reward. It’s just a blast to drive.

Players will start to remember these places on their own.

Little things you want to see through to the end

It could be content that costs next to nothing:

a hot air balloon appears far ahead; for the next ten minutes or so, you gradually get closer to it, then it crosses the road and disappears;
a flock of birds flies alongside the car for a while;
a tumbleweed tries to “race” you;
during a rainstorm, a huge rainbow appears a few minutes later;
a meteor shower begins at night;
a train several kilometers long passes in the distance, even though there’s no railroad track near the player;
an oncoming car flashes its headlights if you flash yours first;
a lone driver stands next to his car listening to the radio; if you stop nearby, he doesn’t ask anything of you;
a small cloud appears above the road, which, for some reason, moves at exactly the same speed as the car for a while.

And very importantly: most of these events shouldn’t yield anything.

As soon as +50 XP appears behind the rainbow, the rainbow ceases to be a rainbow and becomes a resource.

Cars can have not only stats but also personalities

Since you have a lot of cars, I’d give each one a few completely useless features.

On an old sedan, the side mirror wobbles at 80 km/h. In a convertible, the wind affects the radio volume. On some huge American behemoth, the antenna sways slightly over bumps. An old van has a small fan on the dashboard. In a ’70s car, the gauges give off a cozy, dim glow at night. On a cheap clunker, the windshield wipers run at slightly different speeds.

It’s precisely the uselessness that’s valuable here.

Then the player doesn’t think:

“This car has a handling rating of 6.4 instead of 5.9.”

They think:

“Oh, that’s the car with the silly fan.”

I’d especially focus on developing physical gadgets in the cabin.

This could actually become one of the most enjoyable parts of the game.

A dangling keychain. A dog with a bobbing head.

A cup. A cassette on the dashboard. A small toy car. A compass. A Newtonian pendulum. Cubes on the mirror.

They all react to the car’s physics.

The player starts taking turns a little more aggressively on purpose, just to see how everything will fly off to the side. On a long, quiet road, these micro-movements turn the car into a lively little space.

And you can simply place any items you find anywhere in the cabin—no inventory slots or bonuses required.

Very rare “impossible” items

I’d take especially good care of this one.

Let’s say the first few hours of the game look completely realistic. Then, one night, the player sees a huge whale slowly swimming among the clouds.

It’s there for about forty seconds before disappearing over the horizon.

No achievement. No log entry. No explanation.

Another player at the 1,700-kilometer mark might see two suns. Someone else might see a giant arch over the road. Someone else might see a distant city that’s impossible to catch up to. Someone might drive through a stretch at 3 a.m. where the stars are reflected on the asphalt.

And I’d make these things truly rare. Not every 20 minutes, but just enough so that a player would text a friend:

“I just saw something totally weird.”

And the friend might well reply: “That didn’t happen to me.”

This creates the game’s mythology practically for free.

Another system I really like here: the world remembers your journey.

Not progress in the usual sense.

Let’s say there’s a camera somewhere in the car. You can stop and take a picture. At the next gas station or motel, the photo develops like a Polaroid, which you can physically place on the dashboard.

Or the car gradually collects traces of the journey: sand of a certain color in the crevices, stickers from rare gas stations, insects on the windshield, a photograph, a flower found by the roadside.

After 3,000 km, the interior becomes a biography of that specific trip.

Not LEVEL 37, but “here’s a little pebble I’ve been carrying for some reason for the last 600 km.”

It seems to me that this fits your concept perfectly.

And I’d also add one more element that could potentially become the heart of the entire game: the moment of coincidence.

The game knows the music, the time of day, your speed, the road’s shape, and the terrain ahead. Sometimes it tweaks not the physics, but the world’s presentation, just a little, so that everything comes together.

You’re driving at 110. The chorus kicks in. The road reaches the top of a hill. At that moment, a vast valley opens up. The sun peeks out from behind the clouds. A long descent begins. A lone car is driving in the distance.

To the player, it feels like this happened completely by chance.

But the game helped make it happen—just a little bit.

It’s for moments like these—just 15 seconds—that a person is perfectly capable of driving for another hour.

Moreover, I would build the design around this principle: the game never tells the player that something special just happened. No “SCENIC MOMENTS,” no event counters, and no “34/120 seen” collections. The less the game acknowledges the existence of these systems, the stronger the sense of a vast world in which you’re sometimes incredibly lucky to find yourself in the right place at the right time.

And then 40,000 km stops being just an absurdly long distance. A very pleasant thought arises: “I wonder what else I haven’t seen yet?”

**another variant**

The Landscape’s Response to Music
Your idea about the distant decorative layer is spot on, and technically it’s inexpensive: FFT analysis of the audio stream → uniforms in the shader for the fake distant mountains.

Low frequencies—the distant mountains slowly “breathe” and swell.
Mid frequencies—the rocks sway slightly.
High frequencies—stars sparkle and the mirage shimmers above the horizon.
At night, road markings and poles pulse almost imperceptibly in time with the beat (important: barely perceptibly, otherwise it’ll be kitsch).
Power lines sway to the bass. And the hum of the wires themselves (aeolian)—that’s the free magic of the desert road.
Rare events—the “director of moments”
Set up a single scheduler: it will spawn events based on distance/time with high rarity and minimal intervals. 90% of the road is emptiness, 10% is wonder. Candidates:

A meteor shower. A huge moon rising on the horizon. A slow-moving comet.
A distant thunderstorm—a gray wall of lightning on the horizon. You can drive right through it—a brief downpour and that’s it. It feels like “a road just like in real life.”
A traffic light in the middle of the desert that turns green only when you approach it.
A phone booth that rings when you’re nearby. Through the receiver—the wind and someone’s laughter.
A lone chair facing the sunset. A half-buried whale skeleton.
A wooden jump ramp with a sign that says “Go for it”—you don’t have to jump, and that’s the whole point.
Life without pressure
A dog by the roadside—it jumps onto the seat, its ears hanging out the window. The cheapest and most powerful move of all.
Hawks perched on poles, a tumbleweed you can catch up to, butterflies fluttering near the headlights at dusk.
An oncoming car flashes its headlights—flash back, and you’ll get a honk. A tiny social cycle without any AI.
Very rarely—a hitchhiker: 30 seconds of story and a cassette left behind.
Player rituals (interactive is better than passive!)
Events where the player does something are more memorable than mere spectacles:

Stop at sunset, light a campfire, open the hood: the stars are brighter, the radio is quieter, and you can “sleep in” until dawn—the passage of time itself is the reward.
Coasting: Turn off the engine on a long downhill stretch—all that remains is the wind, the hum of the wires, and the clear radio. A little “silence mode.”
Honk in a canyon and hear the echo. Toss a coin into the “wishing well”—a star falls a second later.
Photo frames for sunsets + an album in the glove compartment.
Bumper stickers instead of achievements: “Drove 1,000 km,” “Picked up a dog,” “Saw a whale.” No points, no currency—the reward is the moment itself.
Traces of people
Poles with notes: “Dima was here,” “8,000 more to the sea, don’t give up.”
Humorous signs: “900 km with nothing. This is not a mistake.”
Graffiti on the rocks. Mailboxes in the middle of the desert: optional delivery of a letter to the next mailbox—a cassette or sticker as a reward.
And about the 40,000-kilometer road itself
Think about the promise of the sea. In the first car—a sign that reads “SEE YOU AT THE SEA.” The desert is already changing color around you—so let the water slowly, almost imperceptibly, appear somewhere on the horizon thousands of kilometers away. Then the whole trip becomes a pilgrimage, and the end of the road isn’t a finish line, but a reward: the ocean and the last rays of the sun. That’s the very “special emotional hook” that keeps you driving on.
